## Introduction

Salary negotiation is a conversation about the value and scope of a role, not a confrontation. You do not need to sound aggressive or disclose private financial information. You need a realistic target, evidence for your request, and a clear understanding of the complete offer.

## Prepare before the conversation

Read the vacancy again and list the responsibilities, required experience, tools, reporting line, and location. Then write down three numbers:

- **Target:** the package you would be happy to accept.
- **Floor:** the lowest package that makes the move sensible after considering travel, time, and responsibilities.
- **Stretch:** an ambitious but defensible request.

These are decision aids, not numbers you must reveal. Compare similar current vacancies, ask trusted people in your field for context, and account for your own experience. Avoid presenting an unsourced internet figure as a fact.

## Look beyond the headline salary

Ask whether the quoted amount is monthly or annual, gross or take-home, and whether it includes a probationary rate. Clarify:

- review and increment timing;
- bonuses, commissions, allowances, and overtime;
- insurance, leave, training, and equipment;
- office, hybrid, or remote expectations;
- notice period and any service or repayment clauses.

The best offer is the one you understand in writing. A slightly higher headline number may not be better if the hours, commute, or conditions are substantially different.

## When to discuss pay

If a range is in the vacancy, ask how your experience maps to it. If there is no range, you can ask early enough to avoid wasting everyone’s time, while keeping the conversation open:

> “Could you share the budgeted range for this role? I would like to make sure our expectations are aligned.”

When asked for expectations before you know the full scope, give a researched range rather than a single ultimatum:

> “Based on the responsibilities and my experience, I am targeting a total monthly package in the range of ___ to ___. I am open to discussing the complete package and growth path.”

Use figures you can explain. If you are unsure, say that you would like to understand the role and benefits first.

## Make the case with evidence

Connect your request to outcomes: revenue supported, time saved, systems improved, customers served, projects shipped, or responsibilities you already handle. For a new graduate, coursework, internships, practical projects, and a clear learning plan are useful evidence; you do not need to pretend to have senior experience.

A useful structure is: “The role includes X. I have demonstrated Y. For that scope, I am looking for Z.” Keep the tone calm and factual.

## Responding to an offer

Thank the employer, ask for time to review, and request the offer in writing. If the offer is below your target:

> “Thank you for the offer. I am excited about the work. Given the responsibilities and my experience with ___, is there flexibility to move the base to ___?”

If the base cannot move, ask about a joining allowance, earlier review, training budget, flexible work, or additional leave. Confirm any promise in the written offer, including the date and conditions of a review.

## When to accept or decline

Do not accept under pressure before you understand the employer, duties, schedule, and payment terms. Decline courteously when the package is below your floor, the role changes materially from the vacancy, or the organisation asks for money to secure employment. Never pay an interview or “registration” fee.

## Keep the relationship professional

Negotiate once or twice with a clear reason; do not invent competing offers. Reply by the stated deadline and keep copies of emails. After joining, document your goals and revisit compensation at the agreed review point.

## Final checklist

- I know my target, floor, and reasons.
- I understand gross pay, take-home pay, and benefits.
- I can give two or three examples of my contribution.
- I have asked for the offer and review terms in writing.
- I will not pay money to obtain a job.
